Part 1

What phishing is

Phishing is a message that pretends to come from someone you trust, built to make you hand over a password, a code, money or access. Attackers rely on your reaction in the first few seconds, so the goal of this training is to give you a pause and a checklist.

Email phishing email

Mass messages posing as a bank, delivery firm or IT team, sent to thousands of people at once.

Spear phishing email, chat

A message written for one person, using their name, role or colleagues found online.

Whaling and BEC email

Aimed at executives or finance staff, often a fake order to pay an invoice or change bank details.

Smishing SMS, WhatsApp

Text messages with a link or a number to call, such as a fake delivery fee or account alert.

Vishing phone

A caller claiming to be your bank or IT help desk who pushes you to share a code or install software.

Clone and fake sites web

A copy of a real login page on a lookalike address that records whatever you type.

Quishing QR code

A QR code on a poster, parcel or email that opens a fake page. You cannot read the address before scanning.

Deepfake and AI phishing email, voice, video

AI-written messages, cloned voices and fake faces on live video calls that imitate someone you know.

Part 3

Recognise a fake website

Attackers cannot copy a real company's domain, so they register one that looks close enough. The part of the address that matters is the domain, which sits just before the first single /, read from the right.

  • Read the endIn northbank.example.login-check.example the owner is login-check.example. Everything on the left is just a label the owner chose.
  • LookalikesWatch for swapped letters (rn for m, 1 for l), extra words (-secure, -login) and a different ending (.co instead of .com).
  • The padlockThe padlock only means the connection is encrypted. Phishing sites use it too, so it does not prove the site is genuine.
  • The @ trickIn https://northbank.example@203.0.113.9/ everything before the @ is ignored. The browser goes to the address after it.

Part 4

Social engineering tactics

Phishing works on feelings, not on technology. Each tactic below is meant to make you act before you think.

Urgency

"Act within 24 hours." A short deadline removes the time you would use to check.

Your parcel will be returned today unless you pay N500 now.

Authority

The message appears to come from a boss, the bank, the police or IT. People obey rank without question.

From the CEO: I am in a meeting. Buy gift cards now, I will reimburse you.

Fear

Threats of lost money, a locked account or legal trouble.

Unauthorised login detected. Secure your account immediately.

Greed and reward

Prizes, refunds, easy jobs, or a loan that needs a small fee first.

You won a phone. Pay the delivery fee to claim it.

Curiosity

Subjects like "Is this you in this video?" or "Salary review attached" make you open things.

Staff salary list 2026.xlsx

Familiarity and trust

A real name, a hacked friend account or a copied email thread. Pretexting builds a believable story before the ask.

Hi, it is Tunde from accounts. Following up on the invoice we discussed.

AI-powered phishing and deepfakes

Attackers now use AI tools to write polished messages in any language, imitate a person's voice from a short audio clip, and fake a face on a live video call. A message can look and sound right and still be false, so "it sounded like them" is no longer proof.

AI-written messages

Clean grammar, correct names and a convincing tone, often tailored from your public profiles. Typos can no longer be your main test.

Hi Ada, following up on Thursday's budget review. Can you approve the attached vendor change today?

Voice cloning

A call or voice note that sounds like your boss, parent or friend, asking for money or a code in an emergency.

Voice note: "It's me, my phone is dead. Send the transfer to this number quickly."

Deepfake video

A live or recorded video of a familiar face. In a group call, the other "people" can be fakes as well.

Video call: "This deal is confidential. Do not tell anyone, just wire it today."

How to verify when you cannot trust your eyes and ears

  • Call backEnd the call and ring the person on a number you already have, not one given during the contact.
  • Code wordAgree a private word with family or your team for emergencies. A clone will not know it.
  • Two approversNever move money or change bank details on one person's word, however real they look. Use your normal approval process.
  • Ask something privateAsk a question only the real person could answer. Do not rely on spotting glitches in the video, because good fakes have few.
  • Secrecy plus rush"Keep it confidential and do it now" is a warning sign on its own.
Local note. Banks and payment apps say they never ask for your PIN, OTP, token or card security code, by call, chat or message. Treat any request for them as an attack, even from a number or page that looks right.

Real-world cases

These are short summaries. Open the linked reports for the full details.

2016, US political campaign

Campaign chairman's email account

John Podesta received a fake Google security alert asking him to change his password through a link. Reports say his account was then taken over and thousands of emails were later published.

FearFake login page

Lesson: do not use the link in an alert. Open the service yourself and check there.

Source: Podesta emails (Wikipedia)
2020, social media company

Twitter staff phoned by attackers

Twitter said attackers used phone spear phishing on employees to get access to internal tools, then took over high-profile accounts to post a cryptocurrency scam.

VishingAuthority

Lesson: a caller who sounds like IT is not proof. Hang up and call back on the official number.

Source: NBC News
2013 to 2015, two technology giants

Fake invoices paid by Google and Facebook

A scammer sent invoices that looked like they came from a real hardware supplier. The companies paid more than $100 million in total before it was found. He later pleaded guilty.

Business email compromiseFamiliarity

Lesson: confirm any new bank details or large invoice using a number you already have.

Source: CNBC
2024, engineering firm in Hong Kong

Deepfake video call with the "CFO"

Reports say an employee of the engineering firm Arup joined a video call where the finance chief and colleagues appeared to be present, but were AI fakes. The employee made transfers worth about $25 million.

Deepfake videoAuthority

Lesson: seeing a face on screen is no longer proof. Verify payment requests through a second channel.

Source: CNN Business via ABC17
2013, US retailer

Target breach through a supplier

Reports linked the Target card data breach to a phishing attack on one of its vendors, which gave attackers a way into Target's network.

Spear phishingThird party

Lesson: your security includes your suppliers. A small firm's mistake can open a large door.

Best practices

Tick each habit you will adopt. This list is only for you, nothing is saved or sent.

0 of 9 adopted

If you already clicked or typed a password

  1. Disconnect the device from the network if you opened a file.
  2. Change the password on the real site from a clean device, and anywhere you reused it.
  3. Call your bank at once if you shared card or banking details.
  4. Tell IT or your manager straight away. Speed limits the damage, and reporting is not a confession.
  5. Watch your accounts for sign-ins or transactions you do not recognise.
